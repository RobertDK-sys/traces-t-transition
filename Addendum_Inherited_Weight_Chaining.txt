================================================================================
1. NEW PROPAGATION RULE
================================================================================
In the original algorithm (report, Section 7), each step of the chaining
freely searches for the best pair (N1, N2) -- both bounded by N_MAX = 65536
-- to link the current anchor b1 to a new partner b2 reconstructing the next
target. Only the value b2 was reused as the anchor for the next step; the
weight N1 was re-optimized at every step, independently of what preceded it.

The requested variant modifies this rule: the weight assigned to the partner
found at step n (i.e. N2_n) becomes the FIXED weight of the anchor at step
n+1 (N1_{n+1}), at the same time as b2_n becomes b1_{n+1}:

  Step 1: free search -> obtaining (N1_1,b1_1) and (N2_1,b2_1)
          [b1_1 = starting anchor, method identical to the original report]

  Step 2: {N1_2,b1_2} <- {N2_1,b2_1}, then search for (N2_2,b2_2) such that
          (N1_2*b1_2+N2_2*b2_2)/(N1_2+N2_2) = x0_2, with N1_2 now FIXED (= N2_1)

  Step 3: {N1_3,b1_3} <- {N2_2,b2_2}, then search for (N2_3,b2_3) for x0_3,
          with N1_3 FIXED (= N2_2)

  ... and so on to the end of the list: at every step n>=2, N1_n = N2_{n-1}.

From step 2 onward, only a single degree of freedom remains (N2, a positive
integer bounded by 65536) instead of two: for each candidate b2 in the pool
(opposite sign to b1), we compute
    N2_exact = N1 * (x0 - b1) / (b2 - x0)
round it to the nearest integer (bounded to [1, 65536]), and keep the
candidate b2 that minimizes the reconstruction error. This search is
exhaustive over the entire pool at every step (no shortcut via
continued-fraction approximation is possible, since b2 is no longer a free
variable).

Step 1 of every chain is taken unchanged from the original files
(chainage_beta03.txt / chainage_beta05.txt), in order to guarantee exact
continuity with the previous report; only step 2 onward has been recomputed
with the new rule, on the extended pool (t_max=1000, k_range=15, 60000
candidates), regenerated and independently verified (control value
t=9,k=-3,sigma=-1 -> b=25.092889841584267, identical to the report).

================================================================================
2. MAIN RESULT: PRECISION DEGRADES, BUT REMAINS VERY GOOD OVERALL
================================================================================
The computation was carried out exhaustively over the 8000 starting chains
of the 6 non-empty founding intervals (2000+994+1006 for beta=0.3;
2000+988+1012 for beta=0.5), i.e. approximately 44,000 search steps, each
scanning the entire pool of opposite sign (~30,000 candidates).

Result: removing one degree of freedom (N1 fixed and inherited rather than
freely searched) markedly degrades the typical precision (median around
10^-8 instead of 10^-10 to 10^-13 in the free case), and above all reveals,
in certain intervals, a non-negligible fraction of chains whose error at a
given step greatly exceeds 10^-6 (up to a few % to nearly 0.2-0.3 in the
isolated worst case). The proportion of chains that stay under 10^-6 at
EVERY step varies strongly by founding interval -- from 40.7% to 99.8% --
whereas it was 100% everywhere in the free case:

introduced by weight inheritance therefore genuinely degrades the
achievable precision, but does not eliminate the ability to chain: it
simply makes it less consistently "perfect".

================================================================================
3. WHY THE DEGRADATION STAYS MODERATE: THE ROLE OF THE INHERITED WEIGHT N1
================================================================================
The explanation lies in the order of magnitude of the inherited weight N1
across steps. Unlike N1_1 (always large, since it comes from an optimized
free search), the N1 inherited at later steps is simply the N2 found at
the previous step -- a value that is not optimized on any "stay large"
criterion: it can occasionally fall very low. On the founding interval
beta=0.3 [1], the distribution of the inherited N1 at each step is:

Step | N1 min | N1 p25 | N1 median | N1 p75 | N1 max
-----+--------+--------+-----------+--------+--------
  1  |  2035  | 32949  |   46134   | 56718  | 65531
  2  |   289  |  4688  |    6782   | 27056  | 39169
  3  |    12  |  5122  |   11146   | 16507  | 50315
  4  |    42  |  2203  |    6628   | 26391  | 65529
  5  |    50  |  6521  |   20888   | 39438  | 65527
  6  |   124  |  4752  |   14492   | 25383  | 56756

The observed minimum drops quickly (from 2035 at step 1 to only 12 at step
3), while the median stays on the order of several thousand to a few tens
of thousands. It is this dispersion -- not a simple systematic shift --
that explains the table in Section 2: most chains inherit an N1 still
large enough that the pool of ~30,000 candidates offers, by sheer
combinatorial density, a point falling very close to the target; but a
minority of chains inherit a small N1 (a few dozen to a few hundred), for
which the effective resolution of the search collapses and the error
explodes (up to 10^-1 or more in the worst cases). A moderate negative
correlation is measured between log(N1) and log(error) (coefficient ~=
-0.25), confirming this mechanism.

This result directly extends the critique in Section 6 of the main report,
but in a more nuanced way than expected: removing one degree of freedom
does introduce a real, measurable constraint (it produces occasional
failures, absent from the free case), but it is not sufficient to
systematically turn the problem into a severe test, because the inherited
weight N1 is not constrained to stay small at any step -- it simply
fluctuates, occasionally taking low values. A more severe and systematic
test would consist of explicitly capping N_MAX to a small, fixed value
from step 1 onward (for example a few hundred), so that the constraint
applies uniformly to all chains rather than depending on a random
inherited draw.

================================================================================
6. CONCLUSION OF THIS ADDENDUM
================================================================================
The propagation rule tested here -- inheriting at every step the weight N2
found at the previous step as the new fixed N1 -- introduces a real,
measurable constraint: the typical precision degrades by 2 to 4 orders of
magnitude compared with the fully free case, and above all, a significant
fraction of chains (up to about 60% on the most affected interval, beta=0.5
[4]) fail to stay under the 10^-6 threshold at every step -- a phenomenon
completely absent from the free-weight chaining (100% success everywhere).
With a more realistic tolerance threshold (10^-3), the overall success
rate nonetheless remains very high (>= 99.7% everywhere).

The identified cause is that the inherited weight N1 fluctuates strongly
from one chain to another (from a few dozen to several tens of thousands),
without ever being systematically constrained to stay small: when it
remains large (the majority case), the combinatorial-density argument from
Section 6 of the main report continues to apply almost intact; when it
drops low (a minority but non-negligible case), the precision collapses
locally. To turn this variant into a truly discriminating test of the DMT
framework, one would need to combine weight inheritance with a much
smaller, fixed N_MAX from step 1 onward (for example a few hundred), so
that the constraint applies uniformly to all chains rather than depending
on a randomly inherited draw whose magnitude varies from chain to chain.
